### Recurring high-level lessons

1. **One architectural system beats a set of matching furniture.** Full-height joinery, wall panels and concealed doors should form a continuous spatial spine.
2. **A compact apartment needs one strong object, not many accents.** A monolithic kitchen element or a material portal can carry the identity of the whole project.
3. **Transitions are the luxury.** Shadow gaps, mitred stone, recessed skirtings, flush doors, integrated lighting and clean changes of material create more value than expensive decor.
4. **Low, calm furniture improves perceived volume.** Keep sight lines open and make storage disappear into walls.
5. **Dark colour works best as depth, not paint everywhere.** Burgundy, olive and graphite belong inside niches, on one upholstered piece, or in a single lacquered plane.
6. **Lighting should reveal surfaces.** Grazing light across stone, timber and plaster is more convincing than a ceiling full of spots.

## 2. Three distinct concepts

### A. Walnut Ribbon / Stone Lantern — selected

**Idea.** A continuous smoked-walnut architectural ribbon starts at the entry storage, returns in the kitchen and TV wall, and becomes the bedroom headboard wall. Against it sits one pale but strongly veined stone “lantern” — the kitchen peninsula — repeated only in small, strategic bathroom and living-room details.

**Palette.** Mineral warm-grey plaster; smoked walnut; ivory/grey veined stone; dark bronze; off-white wool and linen; sparse oxblood/burgundy and olive.

**Custom elements.**
- sculptural stone peninsula with one solid end and a recessed walnut seating void;
- living-room portal with recessed TV, floating stone plinth and thin bronze reveal;
- asymmetric bedroom wall combining walnut, upholstered headboard, stone ledge and hidden light;
- boutique-hotel bathroom vanity with full-height mirror, stone niche and ribbed-glass screen.

**Kitchen.** Tall units visually dissolve into one walnut / lacquer wall. The peninsula is the focal object, not a standard table.

**Living.** Low sculptural sofa faces an architectural wall rather than a TV stand. One off-centre coffee table and a quiet rug create layered heights.

**Bedroom.** No two independent bedside tables. The bed sits inside a wall-wide composition with one long ledge and asymmetric vertical elements.

**Bathroom.** Stone reads as large planes. Laundry is visually absorbed into joinery. Mirror and lighting are one integrated object.

**Light.** Warm reflected light at the walnut spine, linear grazing light at the kitchen stone, a single decorative pendant over the peninsula, headboard glow, mirror wash in the bathroom.

### B. Soft Brutalist Salon

**Idea.** Lime plaster and microcement create a continuous shell; smoked oak, brushed stainless steel and deep wine upholstery sit inside it as precise inserted objects.

**Architecture.** A thickened plaster portal around the living zone; stainless steel kitchen plane; ribbed-glass bathroom divider.

**Kitchen.** Stainless-steel / stone work block with a very thin floating timber bar.

**Living.** Built-in plaster bench and one modular sofa; media disappears into a niche.

**Bedroom.** Padded wall with a plaster shelf cut through it; no visible joinery handles.

**Bathroom.** Monolithic microcement with one dark stone vanity.

**Light.** Strong wall wash and reflected ceiling light; decorative lights are almost jewellery-like.

### C. Japanese-European Atelier

**Idea.** Dark oak and rice-paper softness meet European collectible design. The geometry is lighter and more layered than A.

**Architecture.** Timber screens, ribbed glass and slender bronze frames create depth without heavy masses.

**Kitchen.** Floating timber worktop with a stone cooking block; tall units behind a screen-like facade.

**Living.** Low sofa, paper lantern, sculptural occasional tables and a timber screen that also hides storage.

**Bedroom.** Soft textile wall, dark timber shelf, paper-glass bedside light.

**Bathroom.** Pale stone, timber vanity and translucent ribbed glass with almost no chrome.

**Light.** Soft pools, lantern-like decorative fixtures and very restrained technical light.

## 3. Why concept A is the strongest for this geometry

The apartment has a compact, window-poor common zone and several necessary storage volumes. Concept A turns those unavoidable masses into the main architectural idea instead of trying to make them disappear individually. A darker timber spine gives the central zone depth; the light stone object becomes a clear focal point and reflects limited daylight. The same walnut / stone / bronze grammar can move through the entry, kitchen, living room, bedroom and bathroom without every room becoming identical.

The existing plan logic remains intact. The intervention is mainly in millwork, furniture geometry, surface treatment and the visual reading of partitions.

## 4. Implementation decisions for the Three.js model

### Architectural gestures
1. **Continuous walnut spine** — entry storage, kitchen tall units, media wall and bedroom wall use the same timber family and shadow-gap detailing.
2. **Stone lantern** — the peninsula is remodelled as a sculptural object; smaller stone echoes appear at the TV plinth and bath niche.
3. **Bronze line** — thin reveals, rails and ledges are repeated as a quiet graphic datum.

### Room-by-room
- **Kitchen:** remove the “row of boxes” reading; simplify upper storage and make the peninsula a stone object with a recessed timber support.
- **Living:** replace the generic TV console with an architectural portal/plinth composition; keep furniture low.
- **Adult bedroom:** remove the oversized lunar motif; replace it with a wall-wide asymmetric headboard composition and integrated light.
- **Second room:** replace childlike decorative graphics with a more timeless built-in desk / shelf wall, olive textile accent and a soft reading niche.
- **Bathroom:** conceal the laundry visually, add a full-height mirror composition, stone niche and ribbed glass; keep the fittings quiet and dark.
- **Storage:** fewer visible door divisions, thinner shadow gaps, no applied handles where possible.

### Details that should look expensive without expensive materials
- 8–12 mm negative reveals instead of applied trims;
- repeated 30–40 mm radii rather than random curves;
- cabinet fronts aligned to door heads and stone edges;
- one metal finish throughout;
- integrated light sources hidden from direct view;
- curtains full-height and slightly wider than openings;
- stone used in uninterrupted planes rather than many small tiles;
- built-in pieces stopping short of corners to create deliberate shadow lines.

## 5. Design critique after the first pass

The previous model had several strong technical qualities, but visually it still read as “well-made 3D furniture in rooms.” The main problems were: too many separate cabinet faces, generic rectangular furniture, a kitchen assembled as modules rather than composed as architecture, an oversized decorative moon competing with the room, and a lighting layer that behaved more like even technical illumination than editorial interior light.

### Second-pass corrections
- remove the moon and novelty decor;
- reduce the number of visible cabinet divisions;
- make the kitchen peninsula and TV wall the two strongest public-space objects;
- introduce one continuous wall language rather than separate wardrobes;
- make the bedroom asymmetrical and integrated;
- make bathroom mirror, niche and light one composition;
- use burgundy and olive only as isolated depth accents;
- reduce decorative objects and let grain, stone veining and shadow do more work.

## Build principle

**Лучше один очень сильный архитектурный приём, чем десять декоративных. Не украшай квартиру — спроектируй её.**
